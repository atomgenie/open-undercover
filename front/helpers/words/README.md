# The Art of the Word Pair

> A guide to crafting **perfect word pairs** for Open Undercover.

The word pairs are the heart of the game. A great game night lives or dies on the
quality of the pairs it draws. This document explains **what makes a word pair
good**, the game-design theory behind it, the concrete rules for adding one to this
project, and a checklist you can run every candidate through.

---

## 1. How the game uses a pair

Understanding the mechanic is the whole key to designing good pairs. Here is what
actually happens each round (see `components/round/Round.tsx`):

```ts
const randPosition   = Math.trunc(Math.random() * WORDS.length) // pick a random pair
const randUndercover = Math.trunc(Math.random() * 2) as 0 | 1    // pick which slot is "civilian"

const valid          = WORDS[randPosition].fr[randUndercover]     // → all Civilians
const undercoverWord = WORDS[randPosition].fr[1 - randUndercover]  // → the Undercover(s)
```

Three consequences follow, and every one of them shapes what a "good" pair is:

1. **Civilians share one word; the Undercover holds its partner.** Everyone must
   describe their word out loud without saying it. Mr. White gets *no* word and has
   to bluff from what he hears.
2. **The two words are interchangeable.** The engine randomly decides which word is
   the civilian one and which is the undercover one. So a pair must work *both ways* —
   **neither word may be "obviously the odd one out."** If `["Dog", "Wolf"]` only
   feels fair when Dog is the majority word, it is a weak pair.
3. **The pair is the only asymmetry in the game.** Roles are otherwise identical.
   All the tension, bluffing, and deduction is generated by the *semantic gap*
   between those two words. Choose the gap well and the round writes itself.

## 2. The core principle: the Goldilocks gap

A perfect pair sits in a narrow sweet spot of **semantic distance** — close enough to
hide in, far enough to expose.

```
   TOO CLOSE                    PERFECT                       TOO FAR
   (synonyms)                (the sweet spot)              (unrelated)
   ──────────┼──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┼──────────
   Sofa/Couch          Apricot / Plum            Cat / Helicopter
   Car / Automobile    Bee / Fly                 Democracy / Banana
   Big / Large         Match / Lighter           Sadness / Bicycle

   Undercover is         Descriptions overlap        Undercover is
   invisible →           early, then diverge →       exposed on clue #1 →
   pure coin flip,       real deduction,             no bluffing possible,
   no signal.            real bluffing.              no game.
```

- **Too close (synonyms / near-identical):** every description fits both words. The
  Undercover is undetectable and the round becomes a coin flip. *Boring.*
- **Too far (unrelated concepts):** the very first honest clue outs the Undercover,
  and Mr. White can't even guess the theme. *Also boring.*
- **Just right:** the two words **share a category and several attributes, but differ
  on at least one sharp, describable feature.** Early clues ("it's an insect", "it
  flies") apply to both. Later, someone reaches for a distinguishing detail
  ("...it makes honey") and the gap finally shows. *That* moment — the slow reveal —
  is the game.

> **Rule of thumb:** if you can describe one word for ~15 seconds using words that
> *also* truthfully describe its partner, and then run out and have to say something
> that only fits one of them — you have a perfect pair.

## 3. The seven properties of a perfect pair

### 3.1 Shared superordinate category
Both words should belong to the same obvious "bucket": *two fruits, two birds, two
vehicles, two emotions, two buildings.* The shared category is the Undercover's
camouflage. Cross-category pairs (`Apple` / `Democracy`) have no hiding place.

✅ `["Aigle", "Vautour"]` — both birds of prey
✅ `["Ascenseur", "Escalier"]` — both ways to change floors
❌ `["Pomme", "Justice"]` — no shared bucket

### 3.2 A single sharp point of difference
Inside that shared bucket, there should be **one clean axis** on which they diverge —
size, function, danger, habitat, temperature, prestige, era, taste. One axis keeps
the deduction legible; a dozen tiny differences just makes the words feel unrelated.

✅ `["Baleine", "Requin"]` — both huge sea animals; axis = *mammal vs. fish / gentle
   vs. predator.*
✅ `["Barque", "Paquebot"]` — both boats; axis = *scale.*

### 3.3 Symmetry — both words are equally "describable"
Because the engine assigns roles randomly, **each word must be a viable majority
word.** Both need enough concrete, sayable attributes that a table of Civilians can
comfortably describe it. Avoid pairing a rich, evocative word with a thin, abstract
one.

✅ `["Bière", "Vin"]` — both give players plenty to say either way
⚠️ `["Chien", "Existence"]` — one is describable, the other is a philosophy seminar

### 3.4 No accidental one-way "tell"
Check that neither word has a signature attribute so unique that stating any honest
clue instantly reveals which side you're on. If the *only* interesting thing about a
word is a property its partner completely lacks, the gap is too wide.

### 3.5 Common, culture-appropriate, unambiguous
Players describe from a shared mental image, so both words must be **widely known** to
your audience. Obscure words break the round — half the table doesn't have a picture
in their head. Avoid words with multiple meanings unless the ambiguity is the point.

✅ `["Astérix", "Tintin"]` — both instantly recognizable (in a French-culture context)
❌ A regional dish 5% of players have heard of

### 3.6 Roughly matched in specificity and register
Pair concrete with concrete, abstract with abstract, proper noun with proper noun.
`["Batman", "Spiderman"]` works because they're peers. `["Batman", "Superhero"]` does
not — one is an instance, the other its category, so the general word can truthfully
"agree" with every clue and never gets caught.

### 3.7 Fun to argue about
The best pairs create **table drama** — moments where two players give clues that
*seem* compatible but secretly can't both be true, and everyone leans in. Pairs that
share a strong stereotype but flip one trait (`["Ange", "Démon"]`, `["Chat",
"Tigre"]`) reliably produce this.

### 3.8 Survive Mr. White
Mr. White has **no word** and, if eliminated, gets one chance to *guess the civilian
word* from everything he's heard — a correct guess steals the win. This creates a
double-bind that good pairs quietly protect: Civilians must reveal enough to find each
other, yet not so much that Mr. White can name the word outright. A well-chosen pair
helps automatically, because the **shared category keeps early clues generic** ("it's
a sea animal") — describing the *bucket*, not the specific word. Pairs where the
civilian word is so iconic that a single honest clue basically *is* the answer give
Mr. White an easy steal. If one clue would let an outsider guess the exact word, the
pair leans too easy on this axis.

## 4. Anti-patterns (avoid these)

| Anti-pattern | Example | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Synonyms** | `Canapé / Sofa` | Undercover is invisible; coin-flip round. |
| **Unrelated words** | `Chat / Fusée` | First clue exposes the Undercover. |
| **Category vs. instance** | `Fruit / Pomme` | The general word truthfully fits everything and never gets caught. |
| **Obscure word** | `Quetsche / Mirabelle` | Players have no shared image; round stalls. |
| **Lopsided describability** | `Pizza / Nostalgie` | One side has nothing concrete to say. |
| **Only-difference-is-a-name** | `Nutella / Pâte à tartiner` | It's just a brand vs. its generic; no describable gap. |
| **Ambiguous / homograph** | `Avocat` (lawyer *and* fruit) alone | Confuses which sense is meant unless the pair fixes it (here `["Avocat","Juge"]` fixes it to "lawyer"). |
| **Spoiler overlap** | `Roi / Reine` | Any gendered clue instantly separates them — too sharp. |

## 5. Recipes for generating great pairs

Reliable patterns that consistently land in the sweet spot:

- **Sibling species:** `Bee / Fly`, `Eagle / Vulture`, `Whale / Shark`, `Spider / Ant`
- **Same function, different scale:** `Boat / Cruise Ship`, `Path / Highway`, `Ball / Balloon`
- **Same function, rival tools:** `Match / Lighter`, `Stairs / Elevator`, `Butter / Oil`
- **Two members of a set:** `Silver / Gold`, `Beer / Wine`, `Asterix / Tintin`, `Batman / Spiderman`
- **Same role, one trait flipped:** `Angel / Demon`, `Lawyer / Judge`, `Cat / Tiger`
- **Neighbouring places:** `Abbey / Cathedral`, `Bank / Town Hall`, `Bench / Chair`
- **Adjacent foods:** `Apricot / Plum`, `Almond / Walnut`, `Biscuit / Chocolate`
- **Same domain, different medium:** `River / Sea`, `Snowflake / Drop`, `Basalt / Marble`

Every one of these is a real, strong pair already in this database. When in doubt,
start from a category you love and find its two closest-but-distinguishable members.

A few more classic pairs from other Undercover implementations that show the sweet
spot from a different angle: `Sofa / Armchair` (same furniture, axis = *seats one vs.
many*), `Moustache / Beard`, `Apartment / House`, `Magnet / Sticker` (both stick to
things), `Limousine / Truck`, `Football / Basketball`. Notice how each shares an
obvious bucket and diverges on exactly one describable trait.

### 5.1 Categories to mine for pairs

Commercial versions of the game organize thousands of pairs into ~15 categories. They
make an excellent brainstorming checklist — pick one and hunt for two adjacent members:

> Food & Drink · Animals · Objects · Jobs & Roles · Entertainment · Nature & Weather ·
> Sports & Fitness · Technology & Digital · Travel & Places · Relationships ·
> Brands & Companies · Historical Figures · Movies · Funny · Internet Slang

## 6. The data format

Pairs live in [`words-i18n.json`](./words-i18n.json). Each entry is **one pair
translated into all supported languages**. The type is defined in
[`words.ts`](./words.ts):

```ts
export type Language = "fr" | "en" | "es"

export interface WordPair {
    fr: [string, string]  // exactly two words
    en: [string, string]
    es: [string, string]
}
```

A single line looks like:

```json
{"fr": ["Abeille", "Mouche"], "en": ["Bee", "Fly"], "es": ["Abeja", "Mosca"]}
```

### Formatting rules

- **All three languages are required** (`fr`, `en`, `es`), each an array of **exactly
  two** strings. A missing language will show up empty in that locale.
- **Keep the two slots aligned across languages.** Index `0` must be the *same word*
  in every language: `fr[0]="Abeille"`, `en[0]="Bee"`, `es[0]="Abeja"`. Don't swap the
  order between languages.
- **Capitalize** each word (the UI shows them as-is).
- The file is **sorted alphabetically by the French word.** Insert your pair in the
  right place to keep diffs clean.
- One pair per line, `4-space` indent, trailing comma on every line **except the
  last** — this repo forbids trailing commas at the end of a JSON array. Match the
  surrounding style (see [`.prettierrc.js`](../../../.prettierrc.js)).
- **No duplicates.** Before adding, search the file for both words.

### Translation cautions

The sweet-spot gap must survive translation. A pair that is perfectly balanced in
French can collapse in another language if a translation is a near-synonym, a
false friend, or far more/less common. **Re-run the checklist below on every locale**,
not just the one you drafted in. If a good FR pair has no equally good EN/ES
equivalent, pick different words rather than shipping a broken locale.

## 7. Adding a pair — checklist

Run every candidate through this before committing. If you can't answer **yes** to
all of 1–7, keep tuning.

- [ ] **1. Shared bucket?** Both words belong to the same obvious category.
- [ ] **2. One clean difference?** They diverge on a single, describable axis.
- [ ] **3. Symmetric?** Either word is a fair majority word — the pair works both ways.
- [ ] **4. No instant tell?** No honest first clue exposes the Undercover outright.
- [ ] **5. Both common?** Every player at the table has a clear mental image of each.
- [ ] **6. Matched register?** Concrete↔concrete, name↔name — no category-vs-instance.
- [ ] **7. Fun tension?** You can imagine a "wait, that doesn't add up…" moment.
- [ ] **8. Translated & re-checked?** `fr`, `en`, `es` all filled, aligned by index,
      and each locale independently passes 1–7.
- [ ] **9. Formatted?** Alphabetical position, capitalization, commas, no duplicate.

### Quick self-test

> Describe word A out loud for 15 seconds using only true statements. If **most** of
> them are *also* true of word B, and you eventually have to say something that's true
> of A but **not** B — congratulations, it's a perfect pair.

## 8. Further reading & sources

The principles above are grounded in how the game is actually played and echo guidance
from the commercial and community versions of Undercover:

- [Official rules — Undercover™: Word Party Game (Yanstar Studio)](https://www.yanstarstudio.com/undercover-how-to-play)
  — confirms the core loop: Civilians share a word, Undercovers get a *slightly
  different* one, Mr. White gets none.
- [undercover.gg — word pairs & categories](https://undercover.gg/words) — a large
  community pair database organized by category.
- [antebrl/undercover-word-game](https://github.com/antebrl/undercover-word-game) and
  [Pablo-Rio/undercover](https://github.com/Pablo-Rio/undercover) — other open-source
  implementations that welcome community word-pair contributions.
- [Imposter/Undercover how-to-play tutorial — Game On Family](https://gameonfamily.com/blogs/tutorials/imposter)
  — reinforces the guiding idea: the two words must be *close enough to bluff with, yet
  distinct enough for Civilians to eventually tell apart* — the Goldilocks gap.

The consistent takeaway across all of them matches Section 2: aim for the sweet spot of
semantic distance. Everything else in this guide is a way to hit it reliably.
